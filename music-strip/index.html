What remains when the song fades? The story of the Bangalore Music Strip

Open the live experienceRead the project

The strip

An open-air jam under the trees of Cubbon Park.

In the 1980s, a few friends and music lovers began jamming in Cubbon Park, Bengaluru. For three to four years the strip became the weekend thing to do, with bands, musicians and individuals playing under the trees by the path near the Queen Victoria statue. When it ended, for reasons never clearly known, its influence kept travelling through Freedom Jam, Take 5 and the scene that followed. This project reimagines the strip as an open archive.

Aim

To design a new media public-space installation and experience for Science Gallery Bangalore as part of the Sci 560 exhibition, using the principles of Digital Humanities on public-space performance, and drawing a parallel narrative to the Bangalore Music Strip.

01

Reading around it

Secondary research set the frame before any interview. It had two strands: how the world treats street performance, and how rock arrived and grew in India.

Busking worldwide

156+ busking and street-art festivals dedicated to such artists exist around the world. Performances rely on audience donations rather than ticket sales, which makes them an open, accessible platform for performers.

Colonial ports and the Anglo-Indians

Western music entered through port cities like Mumbai and Kolkata. Bangalore was not a port but built its own vibrant scene. The Anglo-Indian community acted as cultural mediator, popularising swing, jazz and early rock.

Emergency and Woodstock

The 1975-77 Emergency suspended civil liberties, enforced censorship and banned public gatherings, which deepened the need for communal spaces. Woodstock 1969 carried peace and freedom ideals that resonated with Indian youth.

The research map branched into: Bangalore's brief history of rock, media and technology, regulation, control and freedom, influence on Indian youth, music culture and movements, adaptation of other Western genres, liberalisation and the evolution of Indian rock, and busking and regulation in India and Bangalore.

Reading the archive

Five archival images, the available materials, were read with the AEIOU framework. Together they show a movement that turned public space into a place for spontaneous, community-driven music in the 1970s and 80s, from full-band electric sets with drums and microphones to intimate acoustic jams, part casual improvisation and part weekly social ritual.

03

Four events, attended

Four case studies, from intimate settings to corporate extravaganzas, were observed for how musicians, audience and space interact.

Sangeetham

An initiative by Gopal Navale, held every weekend at Sankey Tank, Malleshwaram. A closed group of 6 to 10 practises and sings together for an hour, training classical and semi-classical voice. People sit in a semicircle under the trees, with almost no boundary between performer and audience, and park sounds mixing in.

Beautiful Spaces Intimate Places

An initiative by Konarak Reddy for intimate concerts of limited size: about 50 people, in venues across the city, for 3 to 4 hours of rock and Western-Indian fusion. Warm lighting, close seating, and musicians talking with the audience between songs.

Kanta Music Festival

An initiative by Blr Hubba to make the city's culture more prominent and accessible. A free event at Freedom Park with 500 to 800 people on average, three or four bands a day over 15 days, mostly rock.

Bandland

A corporate festival by Book My Show and RNA at Nice Ground, Madavara: about 10,000 people, eight hours, around ten bands a day, mostly rock, tickets from 5k. Open grounds, a conventional stage and mosh areas.

The pattern is clear. In small, participatory events the boundary between performer and audience dissolves and people share authorship of the experience. In large events the focus moves to spectacle, technology and staging do the work, and the relationship becomes largely one-way, sometimes at the cost of deep connection. Space, social proximity and the performer-audience boundary decide how music is received and remembered.

04

What the strip left behind

The strip seeded a mindset about music's place in Bangalore. Its ripples can be traced in several initiatives.

Freedom Jam

An initiative by Harish Chouthay to bring back the Sunday jam spirit of the strip, as an open jam environment.

Take 5 and Beautiful Spaces

Take 5 is among the pub-based live stages that pioneered live performance in the city. Beautiful Spaces Intimate Places is run by Viroj Suvarna.

Music Strip Reconnect

Started by Gopal Navale, Srinidhi and Sidhart Patnaik from the strip, and continued till now by Sidhart Patnaik.

There is a growing desire to reconnect with what the strip stood for: reclaiming streets as cultural spaces, breaking down elitist barriers, and valuing spontaneous, community-led expression. As cities become more privatised and regulated, even a symbolic revival helps preserve a public cultural commons and lets a new generation see music as social glue.

05

Thirty-three voices

Audio interviews with 33 people produced around 30 hours of recordings. They included participants of the strip remembering the good old days, an organiser of the strip, musicians on the 90s scene and today's, the owners of Koshy's, a saxophonist, a journalist on public space, band members of Thermal and a Quarter, and the organiser of the Kanta festival. Topics ranged from oral histories and the strip's evolution to the Emergency, social media and current issues.

Eight stories were curated as video interviews that cover the themes found in the audio and the preliminary research.

06

Nine themes, three boundaries

Months of living with the material, on a room-sized Miro board, produced nine themes.

  1. Emergency and its socio-political impact
  2. Colonial legacies: the influence of Anglo-Indians and Western music
  3. Hippie movement: from the Vietnam War to India's rock scene
  4. Music, drugs and the psychedelic era
  5. Community, creativity and the need for shared spaces
  6. Culture vs. anticulture: the limits of public expression
  7. The politics of noise: controlling public spaces
  8. Myths of Bangalore: why it became India's rock capital
  9. Corporate sponsorship and the decline of indie culture

One idea ties them: boundaries, physical, cultural and societal, that shaped the scene, and how they were identified, enforced and eventually transcended through music, creativity and community.

Boundaries of locality

Cantonment, garages and Cubbon Park in fostering rock culture, and the divide between cosmopolitan Cantonment and more traditional parts of the city. Public and semi-public music spaces evolved from open spaces in the 1920s to the strip of the 1980s and today's commercial venues.

Boundaries of culture

The tension between Western and Indian influences, the Anglo-Indian hybridisation, and counterculture against conservative norms in the 70s and 80s. Who decides what art is acceptable in public, and what counts as Indian.

Boundaries of third space

Music as resistance during the 1975 Emergency and other tense periods, the decline of free grassroots performance under commercialisation, and the ongoing contest over who controls public space: musicians, authorities or commerce.

A first idea: the anthology vinyl

Ideation began with a vinyl: an anthology album that sets archival interviews and audio narratives against reinterpretations of iconic rock songs from the 1960s to the 1990s. It was meant as a storytelling artifact, not just a listening medium, and it gives new artists room to reimagine the classics.

Audio narrative

Short stories from the anthology, told in spoken word.

Context and scene

Setting the scene through radio announcements, with lyrical correlation and meaning.

Famous rock songs

Songs of the past as familiar anchors, covered by new artists.

One pairing: the story of Sunbeam Motha laying the foundation of the strip, set with With a Little Help From My Friends (The Beatles, 1967). The thinking about eras and objects, a record for the 70s, grew from here.

09

The final direction

The walkthrough is not a linear progression. Stories are reached through the city's streets, landmarks and music venues, and the city itself remembers: some places carry ghosts of past performances, some change over time, some disappear. Like the Ship of Theseus, the memory of the strip alters with each telling, and as more stories and perspectives are embraced, more colours fill the lines and the illustration grows more vivid.

Anthology

Each story stands alone yet interweaves with the others. Characters, locations and recurring themes link across decades.

Sound

Diegetic and non-diegetic sound shape the experience: street noise, music bleeding into scenes, public announcements.

Music over time

Music evolves with the timeline: psychedelic rock in the 70s, underground indie in the 90s, digital music in the present.

Four musicians walk through the experience as guides. They are drawn from photographs in the strip's archives and inspired by the Abbey Road album cover.

10

Designing the sound

Short, iconic riffs from historically significant songs act as sonic triggers. They anchor each story in its time and guide the listener emotionally. A few bars of With a Little Help From My Friends, for instance, stand for collective energy and communal space.

Deconstruct

Riffs are never played whole or used as standalone music. They are taken apart and layered into custom compositions, in the spirit of musique concrete.

Re-record

Cover musicians re-record the riffs, adding interpretive variation. They sit beneath new instrumentation.

Resemble, not copy

Duration and modulation techniques stress thematic resemblance over direct duplication, which also addresses copyright limits.

Each frame also has its own ambience: Cubbon Park, mild traffic and people chatting; a metal door closing with a heartbeat; birds and a modified radio version of Magic Bus; street sounds, construction and vendors bargaining; metro announcements; and a walk down Church Street where songs from the clubs shift like a doppler effect as you move.

11

Drawing the city

The cityscape was sketched in Procreate, then iterated through moodboards, three composition studies and a final style. It is divided into parts that run left to right across time, with landmarks labelled on the map: Vidhan Soudha, Hard Rock Cafe, LIC, Koshy's, Deccan Herald, Plaza theatre, Freedom Park, the jail, the hippie trail, the High Court, the King Edward statue, Cubbon Park and the Queen Victoria statue. A story's era object appears as a surreal, coloured element that does not fit the pen drawing, inviting the user to interact.

12

From 2D to 3D

To make it more intuitive and immersive, a 3D spatial approach was adopted and drew very positive feedback in testing. The sketches became 3D planes in Blender, arranged in an octagon showing the eight stories. The camera sits in the centre and pans from one side to another with a zoom-in transition to frame each story. The interactive site was built with Blender and Theatre.js, with layered sound and music behind it.

13

Testing it with people

Participants explored three iterations and were asked to navigate, interact with objects and comment on clarity, usability and engagement.

Iteration 1

A 2D world with horizontal scroll. Felt simple and limited in engagement.

Iteration 2

A 3D world with numbered buttons, 1 to 19, for navigation.

Iteration 3

A 3D world with scroll provision. Rated the most intuitive.

What worked

  • Users could scroll through and explore the illustration
  • The central TV and windows were noticed as a narrative anchor
  • The choreography of transitions, when visible
  • Background illustrations, animation and the 3D view
  • Positives moved from noticing features to enjoying smoother storytelling

What didn't

  • Buttons lacked clarity of affordance; users unsure where to click
  • Scroll speed and the scroller design felt unintuitive, and interaction felt flat
  • Photo frames lacked context: unclear who they represent
  • Text legibility problems, and real-to-memory transitions not clearly visible
  • Too many instructions on the first page, too few later, and some zoom jumps still jarring

Insight: people are willing to explore, but need clearer cues for what is clickable, and the narrative metaphor of real to memory is not yet translating strongly.

Affordance

Consistent glow, colour and hover effects on interactive objects. Simplified, standard buttons.

Narrative clarity

Smoother real-to-memory transitions. Explicit scroll and zoom cues. Labels or hover previews on photo frames.

Instructions and visuals

Fewer instructions up front, spread through the journey, with an optional help icon. Better text contrast and softer background layers.

14

The web experience, and what it was made with

The experience is hosted on the Sci560 legacy website, where a year of exhibits appears as a map of Bangalore. Zooming toward Cubbon Park reveals the music strip icon. Its pages: a loading page, a quote page, an instruction page, then a room. The room holds artifacts for the eight stories (a no-musician sign on the wall, an old newspaper about the Emergency, a small hippie van toy), a CRT TV that plays an opening video with acknowledgements for the participants, and a photo wall of research photos and transcript excerpts. Interacting with the window moves the user into the illustration, where the walkthrough runs horizontally. Clicking an artifact starts a story, such as the hippie van opening the animated third chapter about the journey to Germany.

Interviews

Recorded in Voice Memos on iOS and Dolby On on Android; transcripts made with Turboscribe, an AI transcription tool.

Video B-roll

AI video platforms (KREA, Runway ML) generated realistic clips from limited photographs, edited in Premiere Pro to remove irregularities.

Illustration

Sketches made in Procreate, a model trained to a chosen style to add detail, stitched together with filler elements from generative AI in Photoshop.

Sound and music

Background tracks in GarageBand, MIDI keyboard via Logic Remote on iPad, ambient sounds recorded in Voice Memos, extra effects from Envato, mixed in Premiere Pro.

Website

UI designed in Figma, prototyped in Readymag, 3D space built from photos in Blender. Moved to VS Code, with GitHub Copilot, for flexibility that Readymag and Webflow lacked.
