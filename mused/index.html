The idea

  • One colour for each note. After repeated use, a learner links a sound to its colour.
  • Notes that fall. The music sheet turns into falling blocks, so you can see what comes next.
  • Colour that fades. The levels remove the colour step by step until the learner no longer needs it.
  • Notes, scales and chords through a song. Pick your favourite song and learn through it.

01 · Problem

Learning an instrument alone, without anyone to say if it is right

Fact file
1problem statement6learner frustrations3"how might we" questionsPianothe instrument in focus

Lockdown turned many people into self-learners. Cost, a flexible schedule and your own pace all work in favour of teaching yourself. But with no one nearby, the feedback is gone. Learners cannot tell if they are making progress, and the sense of achievement fades. Favourite songs keep people going, yet the sheets for them are often not available, and preset songs run out of charm quickly.

Products that light up the keys help with the order of notes. But music is also about how hard you press, how the rhythm sits and whether the tempo is right. And an expensive built-in system is out of reach for most people.

Effective learning experience of musical instruments in an unaided environment.

No instant feedback from a source they trust

Without timely guidance, learners feel adrift. They want someone to confirm progress and correct mistakes.

Cannot play their favourite songs

Complexity or missing resources keep people from the songs they love, and that cools their passion.

No sense of satisfaction

When goals feel out of reach, the sense of accomplishment fades into frustration.

Lack of proper posture

Without guidance, bad habits form that slow progress and can cause discomfort or injury.

Rhythm issues

A steady rhythm is hard to keep, which leaves playing disjointed.

Following the beat

Playing in sync with the beat is basic to performance, but hard to learn alone.

Online apps and tutorials also paint a picture of easy success within days. A beginner sees a polished "day 1, day 3" video and expects the same, then meets the real pace.

How might we

  • What can keep the user motivated enough?
  • What can make the user understand concepts of proper playing, and not just the sequence?
  • How can the solution help the user comprehend the conventional system, the music sheet?
Why it matters to Mused

This is the brief. It names the user (beginners and self-learners), the instrument (piano), and the approach (multisensory), and it rules out a new instrument: the solution has to work with the one already at home.

04 · Background research

How a question about noise became a question about learning

  • Noise in shared spacesGroups playing their own music in public spaces, disturbing others. I imagined an invisible bubble that contains sound, like a physical discord server. Sound masking in existing products such as Hushme and Muzo was not effective yet, so I set it aside.
  • Comfort zonesResearch on mental well-being in community spaces showed that people go out with a fixed comfort zone and often do not feel they belong to the space.
  • Zoning in and outI turned to how the mind drifts. Highway hypnosis, the trance-like state of long, repetitive drives, appeared in user incidents and in a study by the National Highways Authority of India.
  • FlowThe opposite state is flow: full focus, with the challenge matched to the person's ability. I read about micro-flow experiences that a design can create on purpose.
  • Hobbies and musicHobbies can trigger flow. A friend in flow while listening to a colleague sing made me want to learn an instrument and reach the same state.
  • The pandemicMany took up an instrument and shops ran short of stock, as reported at the time. Learning moved online, where the feedback was missing. That became the question: an effective music learning experience in an unaided environment.

Flow sits in a narrow band. Too much challenge for your ability gives anxiety. Too little gives boredom.

A beginner who cannot tell what to do next drifts to the anxious side. A beginner who only follows lights drifts to the bored side.

Why it matters to Mused

The route from noise to flow explains the design goal: keep a learner in the narrow band where the task is just hard enough. The levels in the player work that way. Beginner shows everything, Amateur fades the colour, and Expert takes it away.

Across all twelve, three gaps kept coming up:

Just sequencing?Lack of finger positioningBland music library

One more thing from the study: the multisensory side has a stronger effect on the user than the sound, so the sound becomes the second signal and colour the first.

What the precedents are missing

The ONE works only on 88-key instruments. It shows the order of notes, but little about rhythm, beat or finger placement. After a while, users sense there is more to music than following a sequence.

Screen-based products have limits. The grey areas that apps cannot solve are still unsolved by the physical ones, and solutions made for self-learners are not much explored.

Why it matters to Mused

Mused keeps the best of The ONE, a strip that fits the piano you have, and borrows colour from Specdrum and the finger idea from Tap. Then it adds what none of them do: the finger, the beat, and a way to learn to read the sheet.

06 · Music and colour

People have been matching notes to colours for three hundred years

Fact file
1700swhen colour and sound were first matched1915Scriabin, Prometheus: Poem of Fire12hues in the pitch-to-colour palette7hues Mused keeps, one per natural note

Ideas for matching colour with sound go back to the 1700s. Some people hear colour when they hear sound. This is called synaesthesia, and for coloured hearing the people are called chromesthetes. The research idea is to give non-synaesthetes a similar link by repeated practice, which I call artificial chromesthesia.

In 1915 the composer Alexander Scriabin took a keyboard with lights, the clavier à lumières, to the New York premiere of Prometheus: Poem of Fire. Pressing a key sent out a different colour of light. The link most often proposed by researchers is pitch to hue, and several light instruments already use it.

A pitch-to-hue palette cited in my report, from C to B:

CredC#orange-redDorangeD#yellow-orangeEgreen-goldFyellow-greenF#greenGgreen-blueG#blueAviolet-blueA#violetBviolet-red

Mused simplifies this to seven hues, one for each natural note, and the player above uses them.

Why it matters to Mused

This is the base for the colour for each note. Test 1 in the journey below checked it with ten people: colours helped, and too many signals at once did not.

The journey

The reading is above. This is the rest of the path: visits, interviews, tests and building, in order.

Fact file
2music academies visited in Gandhinagar2user tests, with 10 people in Test 111steps, from lockdown to the build5days of colour-for-notes testing
  1. Step 1

    Lockdown made a lot of new learners

    In lockdown many people took up an instrument to break the sameness of the days. A Public First study reported by The Guardian found around one million UK adults had taken one up. Most had no teacher close by. They searched for their favourite songs online and started to teach themselves.

    That is where the trouble starts. Nobody is there to say if a note was right, or if the hand sits well, so the early excitement fades.

  2. Step 2

    Visiting two music academies

    I visited two music schools in Gandhinagar. Both moved online. Harmony Music Academy, which teaches keyboard and guitar to all ages, stopped classes for a while. Teaching online did not work well there: poor connections, clashing schedules, and no direct feedback from the teacher.

    Mihir Guitar Classes carried on without a break, but a song took longer online, and the teacher could not show how to hold the instrument or place the fingers. In both, money mattered most. Students who could not buy an instrument for home, or rely on their internet, left sooner.

  3. Step 3

    Listening to self-learners

    A 24-year-old master's student, magician and music enthusiast, learned guitar from videos. “Knowing when to press, where to press would have solved many issues. Looking at the screen, looking back to and fro is difficult.”
    A 26-year-old master's student whose friends encouraged her to learn. Online videos left her unsure if what she played was right. That feeling slowly killed her enthusiasm.
    A student with a year of keyboard lessons at school. He learned favourite songs from chord sites, recorded himself, and asked friends and family for feedback. After the pandemic he started a college band.
    A school student who learned by ear, note by note. Remembering the notes got hard and the fun faded. The instruments now sit around as decoration.

    What they shared: favourite songs kept them going, and what was missing was quick feedback from a source they could trust.

  4. Step 4

    Mapping what already exists

    The full study is in the secondary research above. I mapped products on the market: Specdrum, the Wave ring, the Oria Enhancia ring, the Tap wearable keyboard, The ONE Hi-Lite and the AUUG Motion Synth. The ONE Hi-Lite stood out because it turns any 88-key piano into a smart one without buying a new instrument.

    But light-up keys mostly show the order of the notes. They say little about the beat, how hard to press, or where the fingers go, and screen apps have limits of their own. That gap became the brief.

  5. Step 5

    Sketching the idea

    I tried different kinds of feedback. Sound can clash with the music being played. Vibration works as a second signal. Colour could be adjusted and was the clearest, so I chose visual feedback.

    Keys that press themselves could teach rhythm, the way a baby learns a language, but that cannot be a universal add-on. Light-up keys can fit any piano, so I went with those and added the finger, the beat and a screen.

  6. Step 6

    Colour for each finger, or for each note?

    I explored two ways to show which finger presses where. One gave each finger a colour, using a glove or a ring. The other gave each note a colour from the visual spectrum, shown on an addressable LED strip.

    Both solve the question of which finger goes where. Colouring the fingers is easier and keeps the solution simpler. Colouring the notes builds the link between colour and note that helps a learner understand the music sheet at a basic level. Since reading the sheet matters, I went with the addressable LED strip, plus a screen that shows the beat and the colours in a pixel-game style.

  7. Step 7

    Test 1: can colour teach notes?

    Ten participants took part over five days, in five levels. I gave each note a colour and projected the colours in time with a song, so the learner could link a sound to a colour. The song was Happy Birthday, because people know the tune.

    Most people could identify the notes after a few tries. Half found coloured dots the best method, and others preferred coloured letters. People with no music background moved one finger at a time at first, then began using several. Repeating the colours built muscle memory.

    Most linked bright colours with higher notes and dark ones with lower notes. Green, yellow and red were remembered better than blue, violet and indigo, and the strength of the colour mattered too. The last level, with colour on the keys as well as the projection, was too much input, and people remembered less. That helped me decide where to stop.

  8. Step 8

    Showing right, wrong and the beat

    Learners also need to know when they are wrong. I sketched feedback with three states: a wrong note, a wrong finger placement, and a correct press, plus a pulse for the beat. In the player above, the line under the keys says if you were early, late or on the beat.

  9. Step 9

    Falling notes for reading the sheet

    Reading a music sheet came out as a key insight from the interviews, so I tried different ways to show it and different orientations. Cascading notes, like the idea of synaesthesia, let a learner see what is coming next in a fun way. The 90s retro games look felt right, with pixel visuals and notes that fall.

    The notes could be symbols from the conventional reading language, or letters in colour. Both seemed worth testing.

  10. Step 10

    Test 2: can people read falling notes?

    Reading a music sheet is one of the hardest parts for self-learners. I turned the staff so the notes fall, like a block game, and asked participants to watch a video of it. Over five trials they had to identify notes and spot them on staves.

    Every participant managed it within five trials. Most chose one note as a landmark and read the others from it. Viewing the staff vertically was easier than expected. The confusion came from notes that repeat in several places. The test suggests that sheet reading can be taught through a game-like format.

\1

Sight reading made better

For people learning keyboard alone, the traditional sheet is hard: the notes, symbols and rhythms are packed together and can overwhelm a beginner. Mused redesigns the sheet so reading it feels like a game.

Before: the conventional sheet
After: the same notes in colour

Artificial chromesthesia links the traditional sheet to Tetris-style pixel games, with colours or shapes for the notes. The sheet becomes a cascading, downward flow that keeps the order of the notation.

  • Comprehending the sheet. Colour makes a complex notation easier for a beginner to read.
  • Colours for notes. Colours for notes, rests and dynamics help learners see how pitches and lengths relate.
  • Gamified. Challenges, rewards and progression levels make practice feel like play.
  • A basic template. A simplified sheet gives a base for building the colour and note link.

From wearable gadgets to a gamified screen

The pitch frames the project as a path from wearable gadgets to gamified visualisations. A light on each finger was the first idea. The strip and the falling notes are where it went.

The app: notes, chords and scales through a song

The app pairs with the strip. The learner chooses a mode, Notes, Chords or Scales, and a favourite song from a large online library, and the lesson is played as falling blocks. Under the modes: singing and matching notes, single-hand notes, chords and scales, improvisation, reharmonisation, notation for both hands, an introduction to the waltz, and separate melodies for two hands.

Where it stands

Mused is a concept with working prototypes and tested ideas. It is not a finished product. The plan has three stages.

Stage 1

Mused

Colours and lights for guiding and navigation.

Stage 2

Project Ghost

A self-playing keyboard that copies a tutor's actions, as an online solution.

Stage 3

More instruments

Guitar, ukulele and violin.

Designed and built by Anjal B, M.Des in New Media Design, NID Gandhinagar. Photos, renders and drawings are from the project.

The songs in the player are public domain tunes. The notes, scales and chord sets are standard music theory. The player is a demonstration of the idea on a screen, and does not read a real keyboard.
